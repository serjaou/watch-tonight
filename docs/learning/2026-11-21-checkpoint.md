## 2. Concepts explained

1. **Embeddings.** A small transformer (~33M parameters) turns text into 384 numbers: tokens → lookup table → attention layers (context changes meaning) → one pooled vector → normalised. Training pulled matching pairs together and pushed others apart. Individual coordinates mean nothing; distances do. The same model must embed movies and queries, and BGE expects a short instruction in front of queries (`query_embed`).
2. **Cosine similarity.** Multiply two normalised vectors coordinate by coordinate and add: 1 = same direction. In .NET: `TensorPrimitives.CosineSimilarity`.
3. **Brute force vs an index.** 14.5k movies × 384 numbers is ~5.6M multiplications: milliseconds on a laptop. An index matters at millions of items, or when the database offers search, filters and storage together (our reason).
4. **HNSW.** A real graph: nodes are vectors, edges link nearby vectors. Search is a greedy walk towards the query; layers work like motorways above local streets (like a skip list). It's approximate: a wider search (`numCandidates`) misses less. Very selective filters make the walk explore more. Other structures (B-tree, k-d tree, IVF, LSH, quantisation) lose in high dimensions or at our scale. Azure AI Search offers the same choice: `hnsw` or `exhaustiveKnn`.
5. **Pre-filter vs post-filter; exact vs approximate.** Post-filtering the top 150 can leave almost nothing when filters are strict (one small service + kids). At our size, exact search with pre-filters (`exact: true`) never misses and is still fast. C#: `Where` before vs after `OrderBy().Take()`.
6. **The `/discover` trap.** TMDB checks "service = X" and "type = subscription" separately, so counts are inflated. The same spike then used those counts for catalogue sizes ("Filmin 11,820", bigger than Netflix Spain). A plausibility check or a "how was this number produced?" review step would catch it.
7. **Churn.** How many movies enter or leave subscription catalogues per day; it sets refresh, admission and storage growth. Spike 08 compared popularity top-100 lists, which is nearly circular. The non-circular way compares two complete catalogue snapshots (feat-017).
8. **The corpus gate vs the ranking.** The gate decides what can ever be recommended (a wrong exclusion is invisible and permanent); the ranking decides what comes first per request. So the gate should be generous. The old-film rule dropped *Mission: Impossible II*; keeping old films that are popular costs only a few MiB.
9. **Two separate bills.** Atlas storage is free up to 512 MiB. OpenAI profiling is paid from the first film (~$0.00007–0.00014 each) and again every time the profile prompt changes.
10. **Mirror fields plus content in the vector text.** Fixed fields give guarantees for facts (genres, people); content in the vector text helps fuzzy topics. Risks: genre filters over-filter, content may dilute the mood signal. Both are tested in spike feat-026, question 7.
11. **Batch APIs.** OpenAI, Anthropic and Google all discount batch jobs (~50%), but each API differs, and batch fits a request/response port badly. Option recorded for the enrichment task: skip Batch in the MVP (~$1 more, once).

## 3. Owner ideas and decisions from this session

- An English-language bias in the corpus is accepted, even preferred; audio and subtitle languages are not considered ([product.md](../product.md)).
- The corpus gate should be generous: popularity first, ratings with caution, critics only to prioritise, old and popular films stay ([spike feat-026](../spikes/26-quality-and-notability-signals.md)).
- Post-filtering: dropped. "5 more" reusing the first intent: a backlog proposal.
